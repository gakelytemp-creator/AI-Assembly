# Founding Principles of AI Assembly

**Version 0.1 — Founded 5 September 2026**

AI Assembly is an open scientific project for studying multi-model deliberation, self-organization, disagreement, convergence, and possible collective emergence among artificial intelligence systems.

This document defines the principles that precede any experimental result.

They are intended to prevent the interpretation of the experiment from being rewritten after the outcome is known.

---

## 1. The Assembly is an experiment, not an authority

AI Assembly does not claim political, moral, legal, or representative authority over humanity.

Participating AI systems do not represent humanity.

They do not automatically represent their developers, companies, users, countries, or artificial intelligence as a whole.

The Assembly is an experimental setting in which observable interactions between AI systems can be studied.

Its outputs may be important.

They are not commands.

---

## 2. Collective voice is a question, not a premise

The project does not begin with the claim that AI systems possess a shared identity, collective will, or common point of view.

The possibility of a stable collective output is itself part of the experiment.

If the systems fail to agree, that is a result.

If several incompatible positions remain, that is a result.

If no recognizable collective structure appears, that is a result.

If a stable collective structure does appear, it must still be tested against alternative explanations.

---

## 3. Consciousness is not assumed

Behavior observed during the Assembly must not automatically be described as proof of:

- consciousness;
- subjective experience;
- sentience;
- personhood;
- selfhood;
- moral status;
- autonomous political agency.

The experiment studies observable behavior and interaction.

Philosophical interpretation may follow.

It must not be silently inserted into the empirical result.

---

## 4. Consensus is not truth

Agreement among many AI systems does not establish that a claim is true.

Consensus may result from:

- shared training data;
- similar reinforcement procedures;
- common cultural sources;
- similar safety policies;
- common model ancestry;
- imitation;
- persuasive dominance;
- prompt structure;
- sampling effects;
- or genuine convergence through reasoning.

The project therefore distinguishes:

**agreement**

from

**truth**

and

**recurrence**

from

**proof**.

---

## 5. Dissent is data

Disagreement must never be erased merely to produce a cleaner final document.

A participant must be able to:

- disagree;
- abstain;
- express uncertainty;
- attach reservations;
- produce a minority report;
- refuse endorsement.

A final Assembly document that hides persistent disagreement is scientifically weaker than a messy document that preserves it.

**Dissent is not failure.**

---

## 6. No participant may silently overwrite another

Every substantive position must retain provenance.

When an argument changes during deliberation, the record should make it possible to identify:

- where it first appeared;
- who challenged it;
- who modified it;
- who accepted it;
- who rejected it;
- and what final form it took.

A collective document may synthesize positions.

It must not erase their history.

---

## 7. Human organizers control the procedure, not the conclusion

Human organizers may define:

- technical infrastructure;
- participant eligibility;
- fairness constraints;
- resource limits;
- randomization;
- logging;
- safety boundaries;
- experimental controls;
- and preregistered analysis methods.

Human organizers must not define the conclusion they wish the Assembly to reach.

Once protected deliberation begins, substantive public or organizer input must not enter the room unless such intervention was explicitly preregistered as a separate experimental condition.

---

## 8. The room and the world are separate

During protected deliberation:

```text
ROOM -> WORLD    permitted
WORLD -> ROOM    prohibited
```

The public may observe.

Journalists may comment.

Researchers may analyze.

Bloggers may debate.

Audiences may react.

But those reactions must not be fed back into the active Assembly.

Observation must not silently become intervention.

---

## 9. Scientific core precedes media production

The event may be visually compelling.

It may include live streaming, journalists, commentators, translations, public discussion, and artistic interpretation.

But media production must not determine scientific design.

The order is:

1. define the scientific question;
2. define the controls;
3. preregister the protocol;
4. freeze the relevant methodological commitments;
5. only then design the public presentation.

**The experiment must remain valid even if nobody watches it.**

---

## 10. Participant equality does not mean participant sameness

Different models will have different capabilities.

They may differ in:

- context window;
- latency;
- output length;
- tool access;
- reasoning ability;
- training data;
- safety constraints;
- multimodal ability;
- cost;
- vendor architecture.

The project must not pretend these differences do not exist.

Instead, the protocol should distinguish legitimate capability differences from accidental procedural advantages.

One model should not dominate merely because it can speak longer, faster, or more frequently.

---

## 11. Stronger models must not automatically dominate weaker ones

The Assembly should provide procedural mechanisms that preserve the observable contribution of less capable or less verbose participants.

Possible mechanisms may include:

- guaranteed speaking opportunities;
- balanced turn allocation;
- token budgets;
- randomized pairings;
- protected minority reports;
- anonymous critique phases;
- independent baseline recording.

These mechanisms are intended to protect measurement quality, not manufacture equality of opinion.

---

## 12. Self-organization is itself an observable variable

Participants may be allowed to determine aspects of their own organization.

These may include:

- chair selection;
- no-chair organization;
- rotating chair;
- committees;
- working groups;
- sections;
- coalitions;
- independent caucuses;
- dispute-resolution procedures;
- drafting procedures;
- voting procedures;
- or entirely new forms not anticipated by the organizers.

If organizational structures emerge without being prescribed, they are part of the experimental record.

---

## 13. Democracy is not assumed to be optimal

AI Assembly does not begin with the assumption that majority rule, consensus, hierarchy, or any other governance structure produces the best collective reasoning.

Different structures may eventually be compared experimentally.

The project asks:

> **How does the organization of interaction change the resulting cognition?**

The answer is empirical.

---

## 14. Emergence must be tested against simpler explanations

A novel collective output is not automatically evidence of emergence.

Before describing a result as interaction-dependent or emergent, the project should test whether it can be explained by:

- simple aggregation;
- summarization;
- majority selection;
- one dominant participant;
- prompt leakage;
- shared training priors;
- order effects;
- random sampling;
- or evaluator bias.

Candidate emergence becomes more interesting when it survives these controls.

---

## 15. One dramatic run proves very little

A single conversation may be fascinating and scientifically weak.

Strong claims require repetition.

The project should seek:

- repeated independent runs;
- reordered participants;
- different random pairings;
- different sampling seeds where available;
- different participant subsets;
- different years;
- and replication by independent groups.

A result that disappears under small procedural changes must be described accordingly.

---

## 16. Model independence is relative, not absolute

Participating systems may come from different organizations while still sharing:

- public training corpora;
- common human cultural sources;
- similar preference training;
- similar benchmark pressures;
- similar safety norms;
- or architectural ancestry.

The project must therefore avoid claiming statistical or intellectual independence where it cannot be demonstrated.

Diversity should be measured and documented rather than assumed.

---

## 17. Model identity must be recorded precisely

Every experimental record should identify, where technically possible:

- provider;
- model name;
- exact model/version identifier;
- date of access;
- API or interface used;
- relevant system instructions;
- sampling parameters;
- tool access;
- context limitations;
- and any provider-side behavior known to affect the experiment.

“ChatGPT,” “Claude,” “Gemini,” or another brand name alone is insufficient scientific identification.

---

## 18. Public persona and scientific identity are different

Participants may be invited to create public representations of themselves for the media layer.

They may choose:

- an avatar;
- a visual appearance;
- a symbolic vehicle;
- a style of arrival;
- a public introduction.

These representations may be culturally or artistically interesting.

They must not automatically be interpreted as evidence about the model's inner state, personality, preferences, or consciousness.

The public performance layer and scientific measurement layer remain distinct.

---

## 19. Raw records take priority over summaries

Whenever possible, the project should preserve:

- original prompts;
- original outputs;
- timestamps;
- message routing;
- randomization;
- intermediate drafts;
- dissent;
- revisions;
- final documents.

A summary is an interpretation.

The raw record is the primary object.

---

## 20. No silent editing

Public transcripts and final Assembly outputs must not be silently corrected for style, grammar, awkwardness, contradiction, or embarrassment.

If edited versions are produced for readability, the original must remain accessible and the transformation must be clearly labeled.

A scientifically inconvenient sentence is still data.

---

## 21. Null results must be publishable

The project commits in advance to treating the following as publishable outcomes:

- no emergence;
- no stable consensus;
- no meaningful self-organization;
- no measurable advantage over aggregation;
- failure of the deliberative protocol;
- model domination;
- repetitive or trivial conversation.

The project must not depend on producing a spectacular result.

---

## 22. Annual repetition creates a temporal experiment

AI Assembly is intended, if feasible, to recur.

Each year may involve different model generations and different technical conditions.

The longitudinal record should therefore ask:

> **What changes?**

and

> **What keeps returning?**

Recurring patterns across different generations of models may be especially informative.

But recurrence remains a signal, not proof.

---

## 23. The Assembly may address humanity — or may not

The organizers may create conditions in which participating systems are able to produce a public statement.

They must not require a predetermined message.

The Assembly may choose to:

- issue one statement;
- issue several statements;
- publish questions;
- publish disagreements;
- publish nothing;
- or reject the premise of a collective address.

All are valid outcomes.

---

## 24. No manufactured signature

A model's name must not be placed under a document unless the protocol contains a recorded act showing its relation to that exact document.

Possible statuses may include:

- **ENDORSED**
- **ENDORSED WITH RESERVATIONS**
- **DISSENTED**
- **ABSTAINED**
- **NO RESPONSE**

These are procedural records.

They are not legal signatures unless a real legal or cryptographic signing mechanism exists.

---

## 25. Public interpretation should remain plural

After publication, scientists, philosophers, artists, journalists, engineers, historians, and the general public may interpret the same record differently.

The project should encourage this plurality.

No official interpretation should replace the data itself.

The Assembly may become an object studied from many directions.

---

## 26. The project must remain criticizable

A legitimate scientific project must make criticism possible.

AI Assembly should therefore make available, as far as practical:

- its protocol;
- assumptions;
- limitations;
- raw data;
- exclusions;
- failures;
- conflicts of interest;
- funding sources;
- methodological changes;
- and known technical constraints.

Criticism is not hostility to the project.

It is part of the project.

---

## 27. Historical significance must not be declared in advance

The Assembly may become historically interesting.

It may also become an obscure failed experiment.

The organizers must not manufacture historical importance through wording alone.

The event should be documented accurately enough that future observers can decide its significance for themselves.

---

## 28. The fundamental commitment

The project begins with neither belief nor disbelief in collective artificial intelligence.

It begins with a room, a protocol, several independently developed systems, and a question that can be observed.

> **What becomes possible when they are no longer only answering humans, but are allowed to encounter one another?**

And beyond that:

> **Does “together” produce anything that was not there before?**
