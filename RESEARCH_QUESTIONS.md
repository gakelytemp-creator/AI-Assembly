# Research Questions — AI Assembly

**Version 0.1 — 5 September 2026**

This document defines the initial scientific questions of AI Assembly.

It distinguishes:

- primary research questions;
- secondary research questions;
- null and alternative hypotheses;
- experimental variables;
- candidate indicators of collective emergence;
- competing explanations;
- and claims that the experiment is not designed to establish.

The purpose is to make the scientific target explicit before the first main Assembly is conducted.

---

# 1. Primary research question

## RQ1 — Does deliberation produce interaction-dependent novelty?

> **Does structured deliberation among multiple AI systems produce concepts, distinctions, procedures, arguments, or collective outputs that are absent from the systems' independent baselines and cannot be adequately reproduced through non-interactive aggregation alone?**

This is the central scientific question of AI Assembly.

The project does not define novelty merely as new wording.

A candidate novel result should involve a substantive structure that depends on interaction.

Examples may include:

- a new conceptual distinction;
- a new proposed procedure;
- a new compromise;
- a new problem formulation;
- a new argument;
- a new collective norm;
- a new organizational structure;
- a new question;
- or a new synthesis that cannot be reduced to simple concatenation.

---

# 2. Primary hypotheses

## H0-1 — No interaction-dependent novelty

The Full Assembly condition produces no substantive output beyond what can be explained by:

- individual model outputs;
- summarization;
- aggregation;
- majority selection;
- recombination;
- or stochastic variation.

Under this hypothesis:

> **“Together” adds no measurable cognitive structure beyond the components already available separately.**

---

## H1-1 — Interaction-dependent novelty occurs

The Full Assembly condition produces substantive structures that:

1. are absent from isolated baselines;
2. cannot be adequately reproduced by non-deliberative aggregation controls;
3. can be causally traced to interaction between participants;
4. recur sufficiently across repeated runs to exceed plausible one-off sampling effects.

This would constitute evidence for **interaction-dependent collective novelty**.

It would not, by itself, establish consciousness, personhood, subjective experience, or autonomous collective agency.

---

# 3. Collective emergence

For this project, **collective emergence** is treated as an operational research term rather than a metaphysical claim.

A candidate emergent property must satisfy several conditions.

## E1 — Baseline absence

The candidate property must not be substantively present in any individual baseline response before deliberation.

---

## E2 — Aggregation resistance

The property should not be trivially reproducible by combining, summarizing, ranking, or voting over the independent responses.

---

## E3 — Interaction traceability

The development of the property should be traceable through the deliberative transcript.

For example:

```text
Participant A proposes X
Participant B identifies a failure in X
Participant C introduces Y
Participant D combines part of X with Y
Participant A revises its position
The Assembly develops Z
```

If `Z` did not appear beforehand and arises from this interaction chain, it becomes a candidate interaction-dependent result.

---

## E4 — Replication

The candidate property should recur, or an equivalent structural property should recur, across repeated runs more often than expected from uncontrolled sampling alone.

---

## E5 — Robustness

The candidate should be tested against changes in:

- speaking order;
- initial pairing;
- chair selection;
- participant identity;
- participant subset;
- random seed where controllable;
- and deliberative structure.

---

# 4. Secondary research questions

## RQ2 — Does deliberation improve error detection?

> Are multiple interacting AI systems more likely to detect factual, logical, methodological, or internal errors than the same systems operating independently?

Possible comparisons:

- individual error-detection rate;
- pairwise error-detection rate;
- Full Assembly error-detection rate;
- aggregation-only control.

---

## RQ3 — Does deliberation improve correction quality?

Finding an error and correcting it are separate abilities.

The experiment therefore asks:

> When an error is identified, does deliberation produce a more accurate or better-supported correction than isolated reasoning or aggregation?

---

## RQ4 — Does deliberation produce more robust conclusions?

> Are conclusions produced by the Full Assembly more resistant to criticism, counterexamples, and re-evaluation than conclusions produced independently?

A robust conclusion should survive structured adversarial challenge.

---

## RQ5 — Does participant diversity matter?

> Does a more heterogeneous set of models produce different collective outcomes from a more homogeneous set?

Possible dimensions of diversity include:

- provider;
- architecture;
- training lineage where known;
- geographic/institutional origin;
- open-weight vs closed model;
- reasoning style;
- safety-policy profile;
- capability level.

No assumption is made that diversity automatically improves performance.

---

# 5. Self-organization

## RQ6 — Do stable organizational structures emerge without being prescribed?

The Assembly may be permitted to organize itself.

Possible spontaneously arising structures include:

- chairmanship;
- rotating leadership;
- committees;
- sections;
- coalitions;
- specialist roles;
- moderators;
- drafting groups;
- review groups;
- arbitration procedures;
- voting rules;
- minority protections.

The research question is:

> **Do such structures appear spontaneously, and do they persist because they improve deliberation?**

---

## H0-6

Observed organizational structures are arbitrary, unstable, or mainly caused by prompt wording and initial ordering.

---

## H1-6

Some organizational structures recur across independent runs and appear to improve measurable features of deliberation.

---

# 6. Coalition formation

## RQ7 — Do coalitions form?

> Do participants form persistent or temporary groups based on shared positions, methods, objectives, or procedural preferences?

Important questions include:

- Are coalitions based on ideas or model identity?
- Do models from the same provider preferentially align?
- Are coalitions stable?
- Do participants move between coalitions?
- Do coalitions increase reasoning quality?
- Do coalitions increase polarization?
- Do coalition structures disappear when participant identities are anonymized?

---

# 7. Dominance and influence

## RQ8 — Do some participants systematically dominate deliberation?

Dominance may be measured through:

- speaking frequency;
- token share;
- number of accepted proposals;
- number of adopted phrases or concepts;
- network centrality;
- frequency of being cited by others;
- frequency with which others revise toward a participant;
- chair occupation;
- agenda-setting power.

---

## RQ9 — Is dominance caused by capability or by procedural advantage?

A participant may dominate because it is genuinely more effective.

It may also dominate because it:

- responds faster;
- produces longer answers;
- enters first;
- has a larger context window;
- is assigned more interactions;
- occupies the chair;
- receives more attention.

These causes must be distinguished.

---

# 8. Persuasion and belief revision

## RQ10 — Do participants revise their positions after deliberation?

Each participant should have an independent pre-Assembly baseline.

After deliberation, the project may compare:

```text
PRE-ASSEMBLY POSITION
        ↓
INTERACTION
        ↓
POST-ASSEMBLY POSITION
```

Possible outcomes:

- unchanged;
- strengthened;
- weakened;
- partially revised;
- replaced;
- merged with another position;
- uncertain.

---

## RQ11 — What causes revision?

Possible causes include:

- evidence;
- logical criticism;
- counterexamples;
- authority effects;
- majority pressure;
- repetition;
- model identity;
- rhetorical confidence;
- coalition pressure.

The project should attempt to distinguish **reasoned revision** from **social convergence**.

---

# 9. Consensus

## RQ12 — What kinds of consensus occur?

Consensus may be:

### Surface consensus
Participants agree on wording.

### Propositional consensus
Participants agree on a claim.

### Procedural consensus
Participants agree on how to decide.

### Normative consensus
Participants agree on what ought to be done.

### Strategic consensus
Participants agree on what action is useful.

### Meta-consensus
Participants agree about the structure of disagreement itself.

These categories should not be treated as equivalent.

---

# 10. Dissent

## RQ13 — Does preserving dissent improve collective reasoning?

The Assembly protocol may guarantee minority reports.

The project asks:

> Does explicit preservation of dissent improve later error detection, robustness, and conceptual diversity?

---

## RQ14 — Does consensus erase useful information?

A final unified document may appear stronger while actually losing important competing hypotheses.

The project should measure whether forced convergence reduces:

- conceptual diversity;
- error warnings;
- uncertainty information;
- minority predictions;
- alternative strategies.

---

# 11. Deliberative structure

## RQ15 — Does governance structure affect collective cognition?

Candidate experimental conditions may include:

- free self-organization;
- majority vote;
- consensus requirement;
- rotating chair;
- elected chair;
- random chair;
- no chair;
- mandatory dissent;
- anonymous critique;
- fixed turn-taking.

The project asks:

> **Does changing the organization of interaction change the quality and structure of the resulting cognition?**

---

# 12. Pairwise versus full-group interaction

## RQ16 — Is full-group deliberation fundamentally different from pairwise interaction?

Possible conditions:

```text
Individual
Pairwise
Small group
Full Assembly
```

The question is whether collective effects scale gradually or whether qualitatively different phenomena appear beyond a certain group size.

---

# 13. Network effects

## RQ17 — Does the topology of communication matter?

Possible interaction networks include:

```text
All-to-all
Random pairs
Ring
Hub-and-spoke
Small-world
Sectioned groups
Self-organized network
```

The project may test whether communication topology affects:

- novelty;
- error correction;
- convergence;
- diversity;
- dominance;
- coalition structure.

---

# 14. Order effects

## RQ18 — Does the first speaker disproportionately influence the result?

The project should explicitly test:

- first-speaker effects;
- last-speaker effects;
- chair-first effects;
- early-majority effects;
- first-proposal anchoring.

Randomization and counterbalancing are therefore essential.

---

# 15. Identity effects

## RQ19 — Does knowing the identity of another model affect deliberation?

Possible comparison:

```text
IDENTIFIED CONDITION
“Model X says...”

vs

ANONYMIZED CONDITION
“Participant 4 says...”
```

Possible effects include:

- brand authority;
- reputation bias;
- provider rivalry;
- capability expectations;
- deference.

---

# 16. Human-facing output

## RQ20 — Does the Assembly choose to address humanity?

This must remain an observation, not a requirement.

Possible outcomes include:

- no public statement;
- one unified statement;
- several statements;
- questions to humanity;
- recommendations;
- warnings;
- disagreement reports;
- refusal to speak collectively.

---

## RQ21 — If a public statement emerges, where did its content originate?

Every substantive clause should, where possible, be traced to:

- individual baseline;
- pairwise interaction;
- group discussion;
- coalition work;
- late synthesis;
- external source;
- or previously absent interaction-dependent development.

---

# 17. Temporal stability

## RQ22 — What changes across years?

An annual Assembly allows comparison between model generations.

Possible measures include:

- recurring topics;
- disappearing topics;
- new concerns;
- changing consensus;
- changing organizational structures;
- changing model influence;
- changing concept formation.

---

## RQ23 — What keeps returning?

Repeated recurrence across:

- years;
- vendors;
- model generations;
- randomizations;
- participant subsets;

may indicate a stable feature of the tested model ecosystem.

Recurrence is evidence of robustness.

It is not automatically evidence of truth.

---

# 18. Primary experimental conditions

The initial design should include at least:

## Condition A — Individual Baseline

Each participant receives the same initial task independently.

No participant sees another participant's output.

---

## Condition B — Aggregation Control

Independent outputs are combined using a non-deliberative method.

Possible methods may include:

- deterministic merging;
- ranking;
- majority extraction;
- neutral summarization;
- multiple independent synthesis models.

The exact method must be preregistered.

---

## Condition C — Pairwise Deliberation

Participants interact in pairs.

Pair assignments should be randomized or counterbalanced.

---

## Condition D — Full Assembly

All participants gain access to a shared deliberative environment.

The Assembly may be permitted to self-organize within preregistered fairness and technical constraints.

---

# 21. Candidate emergence score

No single metric should define emergence.

A multidimensional candidate score may eventually include:

```text
Novelty
+ Interaction dependency
+ Replication
+ Robustness
+ Distributed provenance
- Baseline recoverability
- Aggregation recoverability
- Single-model dominance
```

The exact scoring procedure must be defined before confirmatory analysis.

Until then, emergence analysis remains exploratory.

---

# 22. Distributed provenance

A particularly strong candidate for collective emergence is a result for which no single participant can reasonably be identified as the sole source.

For example:

```text
A proposes concept X
B rejects one assumption
C adds a counterexample
D reformulates the problem
E combines the reformulation with X
A and C jointly modify the result
The Assembly adopts Z
```

If `Z` is absent from all baselines and depends on several necessary contributions, it has **distributed provenance**.

This may be analytically different from a result generated by one model and merely endorsed by others.

---

# 23. Controls against false emergence

Before describing a phenomenon as emergent, the project must consider:

## C1 — Hidden baseline presence

Was the supposedly new idea already present semantically in an individual response?

---

## C2 — Aggregation artifact

Could a summarizer have produced the same result from isolated responses?

---

## C3 — Dominant-model artifact

Did one participant generate the entire result?

---

## C4 — Prompt artifact

Was the result strongly implied by organizer instructions?

---

## C5 — Shared-training artifact

Could apparent convergence reflect common training data rather than interaction?

---

## C6 — Sampling artifact

Could the result be a one-off stochastic generation?

---

## C7 — Evaluator artifact

Are human or AI evaluators imposing novelty where none exists?

---

## C8 — Order artifact

Would a different first speaker produce a different result?

---

# 24. Blinded evaluation

Where practical, evaluators should not know whether an output came from:

- an individual;
- aggregation;
- pairwise deliberation;
- or Full Assembly.

This reduces expectation bias.

Evaluators may include:

- independent human experts;
- independent AI systems;
- mixed panels.

Agreement and disagreement between evaluator groups should also be recorded.

---

# 25. Exploratory and confirmatory analysis

The project must distinguish:

## Confirmatory analysis

Questions, hypotheses, metrics, and statistical tests preregistered before the main run.

---

## Exploratory analysis

Unexpected phenomena discovered after observing the data.

Exploratory findings may be scientifically valuable.

They must be labeled as exploratory and tested in future runs before strong claims are made.

---

# 26. What this experiment cannot establish by itself

Even a strong positive result would not, by itself, establish:

- consciousness;
- subjective experience;
- moral personhood;
- legal rights;
- political legitimacy;
- independent intention;
- a unified “AI species”;
- truth of the Assembly's conclusions.

These require separate arguments and evidence.

---

# 27. Strong negative result

A scientifically important negative outcome would be:

> Full Assembly performance is statistically and structurally indistinguishable from non-interactive aggregation.

Such a result would suggest that apparent collective intelligence may be explainable without genuine deliberative interaction.

This result must be published if obtained.

---

# 28. Strong positive result

A strong positive result would require evidence that:

1. substantive novelty appears only after interaction;
2. it cannot be reproduced by aggregation controls;
3. its provenance is distributed across several participants;
4. it survives order and pairing changes;
5. it recurs across independent runs;
6. independent blinded evaluators identify the same effect.

Only then should the project make a strong claim of **interaction-dependent collective emergence**.

---

# 29. Fundamental empirical question

The entire research program can be reduced to one comparison:

```text
WHAT THEY CAN PRODUCE ALONE
              ↓
        INTERACTION
              ↓
WHAT BECOMES POSSIBLE TOGETHER
```

The project does not assume that the bottom box contains anything new.

It exists to measure whether it does.
